# Fingerprints on Every Page

Welcome back to Runtime Republic. Last time, we read the part of a model bill that lets lawmakers work in a public repository without holding an illegal meeting. Today we read the heart of the proposal: the clauses that say no one can act on a bill until its exact text has been public for at least an hour. And we'll look at the small, clever idea that makes the whole thing checkable by anyone: a fingerprint on every page.

The usual note. This is not legal advice, and the draft is not law. It's a model, written to start a conversation. The Revisor of Statutes, who drafts every Kansas bill, would rewrite much of it, and that's how it should work.

Let's begin with a question. How would you know whether the text your representative voted on is the same text you read an hour earlier?

Today, mostly, you'd have to trust. A bill can change several times in a single day. An amendment on the floor. A substitute in committee. A conference committee report late at night. Each version may be posted somewhere, but nothing ties a vote to an exact version in a way an ordinary person can check. If two versions differ by one word, you might never notice.

The draft solves this with three pieces. A home for every version. A fingerprint for every version. And a clock.

Start with the home. The draft's statute creates an official legislative repository, kept by the Revisor of Statutes. Every version of every bill, resolution, amendment, substitute, committee report and conference committee report goes there. The draft sets six requirements. The repository must use an open, non-proprietary standard. It must be owned and operated by the state. It must be free to read, with no registration. It must record, for each version, the time it was posted and its version identifier. It must never delete or alter a posted version. And it must let anyone subscribe, for free, to notices when a measure changes.

Each of those has a job. The open standard means no single company controls the format. State ownership means the record answers to the public. Free reading means no one is shut out. The time stamp sets the clock running. The rule against deleting means history can't be rewritten. And the notices mean you don't have to sit and watch. The repository comes to you.

Now the fingerprint. This is the part that sounds technical, but the idea is simple.

The draft defines a version identifier as a unique code computed from the full text of a version, by a published method, so that any change to the text changes the code. Think of it like a fingerprint. Two identical texts always produce the same fingerprint. Change a single comma, and the fingerprint is completely different. Software developers have relied on this idea for years. It's how a system like Git, the tool behind GitHub, knows instantly whether a file has changed.

Here's why that matters for a citizen. Under the draft, a motion or report must identify the text it acts on by its fingerprint. So the journal doesn't just say the House passed a certain bill. It says the House passed the version with this exact fingerprint. Anyone can take the text they read, run the published method on it, and see whether the fingerprint matches. If it matches, it's the same text, word for word. If it doesn't, something changed. No trust required.

Let's make that concrete. Picture a retired teacher in Salina who follows a school funding bill. At two in the afternoon, she gets a notice that a new version has been posted. She opens it and sees, in red and green, that one sentence about local budgets changed. She has an hour. She calls her senator's office and asks about that one sentence. A little after three, the Senate votes, and the journal lists the fingerprint of the version it passed. That evening, she checks. The fingerprint matches the version she read at two o'clock. She may agree with the vote or not, but she knows exactly what passed, and she had a fair chance to speak before it did. That's the whole idea, in one afternoon.

Now the clock. The draft's joint rule says neither house, nor any committee, may take official action on any text unless that exact text has been posted in the official repository for at least sixty minutes. Official action is defined broadly: introducing a bill, adopting an amendment or substitute, adopting a committee report or a conference committee report, final action, and enrolling a bill, which means preparing the final official copy.

That breadth is deliberate. If the rule covered only final passage, the real changes could happen at the steps it didn't cover. By naming every step where text can change or become binding, the draft closes those side doors.

And what happens if someone skips the clock? The draft is blunt. Any action on text that doesn't match a fingerprint posted an hour earlier is not valid, and the presiding officer must rule it out of order. That's the difference between a rule and a suggestion. A suggestion can be ignored. A rule with an effect cannot.

The draft also takes on today's thirty-minute rule. Under Joint Rule Three F, copies of a conference committee report must be available to members thirty minutes before the report is considered, and two-thirds of the members present can set that aside. The draft changes thirty minutes to sixty. It requires the report to be posted in the public repository, not just handed to members. And it says the two-thirds waiver can no longer waive the posting.

What about emergencies? A flood, a court deadline, a crisis that can't wait an hour. The draft allows it, with two guardrails. First, it takes two-thirds of all the members of a house, not just those present. Second, even in an emergency, the text must be posted, with its fingerprint, before the vote. The motion must state the reason, and the reason goes into the journal. Fast is allowed. Secret is not.

The statute carries the same idea to the Governor. A bill can't be presented to the Governor unless the enrolled text matches a version that's been posted for at least an hour. The Governor's signature or veto must name the fingerprint. And if a bill becomes law without a signature, the repository records that too, with its fingerprint.

So that's the draft. Now let's test it, the same way we did last time.

The first hard question: which fingerprint method? The draft says a published method, but it doesn't name one. That's intentional, and it's also a gap. Methods age. One that's trusted today may be weakened in twenty years. A good law might name a public standard by reference, so it can be updated without rewriting the statute. This is on the list of open questions for the Revisor.

The second hard question: what if the repository goes down? If the clock depends on a website, an outage could block lawful action. The draft doesn't yet say what happens then. A careful version would require backup copies, and might pause the clock during an outage rather than let action go forward on text no one could see. That's a real gap, and it's recorded as a risk in the project's engineering specification.

The third hard question: does the Governor clause fit the constitution? The Kansas Constitution says a bill must be presented to the Governor within ten days after it passes, and gives the Governor ten days to act. An hour of posting fits easily inside ten days. But a lawyer would want to make sure the statute can't be read as blocking a presentment the constitution requires. That's another open question.

The fourth hard question: can a joint rule really hold? Same answer as last time. A joint rule can be suspended or amended by vote. The draft tries to make suspension harder, requiring two-thirds of each house after a day's notice. But a rule can't fully protect itself. That's why the proposal ends with a constitutional amendment, which says plainly that no bill can become law if its text wasn't public as required, and that the rule can't be suspended.

And the last hard question: is one hour enough? For a short bill, an hour is plenty to see what changed. For a four-hundred-page budget, it isn't enough to read every word. But the fingerprints help here too. Because every version is in the repository, anyone can see exactly which lines changed between versions, in red and green. Reporters, advocates and alert apps can focus on the changes, not the whole bill. The hour is a floor. A longer wait for long bills would be a reasonable addition.

Let's recap. The draft gives every version of every bill a permanent public home, owned by the state. It gives every version a fingerprint, so a single changed comma is visible to anyone. It ties every official action, from introduction to the Governor's signature, to a fingerprint posted at least an hour earlier. Action that skips the clock is not valid. Emergencies are allowed, by two-thirds of all members, but the text is still posted first. And there's work left: naming the fingerprint method, planning for outages, checking the Governor clause against the constitution, and making the rule permanent through an amendment.

One update since this essay was first written. The draft now has an outage rule. While the repository is down, the one-hour clock stops. If an outage runs long, a backup copy chosen in advance becomes the posting place. And every outage is logged, with its start and end times.

One last question for you. If you could get a notice the moment any bill changed, which bill would you watch first?
