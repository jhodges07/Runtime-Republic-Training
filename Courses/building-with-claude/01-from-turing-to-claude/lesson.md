Welcome to Runtime Republic. This is a five-part series about one question: what is the best way for a single person, with a laptop, a GitHub account and an LLC, to use Claude to build a real software and phone app business?

Before we talk about workflows and cheat codes, we need to know what we are working with. So today is a short history. Where artificial intelligence came from. Where Claude came from. And why that history should change the way you work.

Let's start in 1950. A British mathematician named Alan Turing wrote a paper asking a simple question: can machines think? He proposed a test. If you talk to a machine through text, and you cannot tell it apart from a person, maybe the question of thinking stops mattering. That idea, the imitation game, has hung over the field for seventy-five years.

Six years later, in the summer of 1956, a group of researchers met at Dartmouth College and gave the field its name: artificial intelligence. They were optimistic. They thought a few smart people could crack it in a summer. They were wrong by about six decades.

What followed was a long cycle of hype and disappointment. Researchers built systems that followed hand-written rules. Expert systems. Logic engines. They worked in narrow, controlled settings and broke the moment the world got messy. Funding dried up twice, in periods people now call the AI winters.

There were milestones along the way. In 1997, IBM's Deep Blue beat the world chess champion, Garry Kasparov. But Deep Blue could not hold a conversation. It could not write a paragraph. It played chess.

The real turn came from a different idea. Instead of writing the rules by hand, let the machine learn the patterns from data. That is machine learning. Around 2012, a neural network called AlexNet blew away the competition at recognizing images, and the deep learning era began. Faster chips, more data, bigger networks.

Then, in 2017, a team at Google published a paper with a now-famous title: Attention Is All You Need. It introduced the transformer, an architecture that could read a whole passage at once and weigh how every word relates to every other word. Almost every large language model you have heard of is built on that idea.

Scale those transformers up, train them on a huge share of the written world, and something surprising happens. They do not just predict the next word. They summarize, translate, reason through problems, and write code. In November 2022, ChatGPT put that capability in front of the public, and the world noticed overnight.

Now, Claude.

Claude is made by Anthropic, a company founded in 2021 by Dario and Daniela Amodei and a group of researchers who had worked at OpenAI. Their founding concern was safety. If these systems were going to become powerful, they wanted them to be honest, helpful, and hard to turn against people.

One of Anthropic's early ideas is worth your attention, because it sounds a lot like your own work. It is called Constitutional AI. Instead of relying only on people grading thousands of answers, Anthropic wrote down a set of principles, a constitution, and trained the model to critique and improve its own answers against those principles. Written rules, governing behavior, checked over and over.

If you have spent decades in aerospace, that should sound familiar. It is a control document. And that is a thread we will pull through this whole series.

The first Claude came out in March 2023. Claude 2 followed that summer, able to read long documents in one go. In March 2024, Anthropic released the Claude 3 family in three sizes, named like poetry: Haiku, the fast and small one; Sonnet, the balanced one; and Opus, the most capable. That naming has stuck.

Through 2024 and 2025, Claude stopped being only a chat window. It learned to use tools. It learned to operate a computer. And with Claude Code, it learned to work inside a software project directly: reading files, running tests, making commits. Today the family includes Haiku, Sonnet and Opus, plus a newer and more powerful tier called Mythos, with Fable as its public version.

So what does all of this mean for you, building a business?

Three things.

First, the old dream of hand-written rules did not die. It moved. The machine learns the patterns, but you write the rules it works under. Your instructions, your project files, your control documents. That is where your leverage lives.

Second, Claude is a collaborator, not an oracle. It is trained to be helpful and honest, but it can be confidently wrong. The history of AI is a history of systems that looked smart until they hit something new. Your job is to check the work.

Third, the timing matters. For most of AI history, building software took a team. Today, one experienced person with clear rules and good habits can do the work of a small studio. That window is open right now, and the people who learn to work this way early will be the ones teaching everyone else.

That is where we are going.

In Episode 2, we will get practical: the best practices for working with Claude, from how you write a request, to how you set up a project so Claude follows your rules every single time.
