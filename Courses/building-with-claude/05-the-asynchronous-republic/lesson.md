Welcome to the final episode of this series on Runtime Republic. We have covered where AI came from, how to work with Claude, the workflows that multiply one person, and the business underneath it. Today we go bigger. What happens when many people, each working with their own AI, build something together, without ever being in the same room or even awake at the same time?

That is asynchronous work. And I believe GitHub plus Claude is the best platform ever built for it.

Let's start with the problem. Most group projects run on meetings. Everyone has to be free at the same hour. Decisions happen out loud and are forgotten by Friday. The person who talks most wins. And anyone with a job, a family, or a different time zone gets left out.

Now picture something different. Your Kansas Property Tax Liberty Bridge project is drafting a property tax bill. You want people across the state to help write it. Some use Claude. Some use ChatGPT. Some write by hand. Nobody has to attend a meeting. Every idea, every draft, and every decision lives in one place, in writing, with a full history of who changed what and why.

That place is a GitHub repository. Think of it as the town hall that never closes.

Here is how to set one up, in five pieces.

Piece one: a shared constitution. Before anyone contributes, the repository needs written rules. You already do this with control documents. A goal and scope. Standing directions, like your rule that the bill has no means testing. How to propose a change. How a change gets approved. This is the document every human and every AI reads first.

Piece two: rules the AI reads automatically. Put a CLAUDE.md file at the top of the repository. Claude reads it before it does anything. Other AI tools look for similar instruction files, so you can point them at the same rules. Now it does not matter which assistant a contributor uses. They all start from the same constitution. That is the breakthrough. You are not coordinating people's tools. You are coordinating the rules the tools follow.

Piece three: issues as the unit of work. Every task or question becomes a GitHub issue. "Draft the definition of a family trust." "Find how three other states handle this." "Check this section against the Kansas Constitution." A contributor picks an issue, works on it with their AI, and reports back. Label easy ones as good first issues, so newcomers have a door to walk through.

Piece four: pull requests as the voting floor. Nobody edits the main draft directly. A contributor makes their change on a branch and opens a pull request. That is a proposal, with a clear before and after. Others comment. Automated checks run, the same control document checks from your template. Named reviewers approve. Only then does it merge. Every amendment has a sponsor, a debate, and a recorded vote.

Piece five: a cadence. Asynchronous does not mean no rhythm. You have been designing exactly this for Lean Civics: two sessions a year, plus a few virtual session days each month. Use that. Open a call for proposals at the start of a cycle. Close it on a set date. Review and merge on a set date. Publish a summary. People can contribute whenever they like, but they know when decisions land.

Now, how do you teach people to work this way? Because this is the real opportunity. Most people have never used GitHub. That is your next course, and your next consulting offer.

Here are the habits to teach.

Write it down. If it is not in the repository, it did not happen. Decisions go in the issue, not in a side text message.

Write for the stranger. Assume the next reader has never met you and lives three time zones away. Say what you did, why, and what is still open.

Hand off cleanly. When you stop, leave a note: done, not done, next step. Your AI can draft that note for you in seconds.

AI drafts, humans decide. Let Claude research, draft and check. But a person approves every merge. That keeps trust high and keeps the work accountable.

Keep agents in their lanes. Your AI Coordination Service already works on this problem: a shared ledger, where each project and each agent has its own identity, so work from one project never bleeds into another. As more AI assistants work side by side, that kind of routing becomes essential.

And make it open. Your essays are licensed so others can share and build on them with credit. Open work invites contributors.

Let's bring the whole series together.

AI went from hand-written rules, to learned patterns, back to a new kind of rule: the instructions we give the machines. Claude rewards people who write clear rules, work one change at a time, verify everything, and keep humans in control. Those habits turn into workflows, cheat codes and automations that let one person run like a studio. Underneath it, a clean and current LLC carries the apps, the consulting, and the publishing. And the biggest idea of all is that the same discipline lets many people, each with their own AI, build something together, in the open, on their own schedule.

You spent a career in a field where nothing flies unless the documents are right. That discipline is exactly what this new era needs. The people who pioneer it will not be the ones with the fanciest prompts. They will be the ones with the best control documents.

So start a repository. Write the constitution. Open the first issue. And invite someone in.
