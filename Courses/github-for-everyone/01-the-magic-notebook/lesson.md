Imagine you and your friends decide to build the biggest, most amazing Lego city ever. There's just one problem. You live in different houses, in different towns, and some of your friends live in different countries. How do you all build the same city at the same time?

That's the kind of problem GitHub solves. Not for Lego, but for computer programs.

Let's start with something simple. Every app on a phone, every video game, and every website is made from instructions. These instructions are called code. Code is like a recipe that tells a computer exactly what to do, step by step. "Show a button here. When someone taps it, play a sound. Then show a smiley face."

Some programs are small, like a recipe for a peanut butter sandwich. But some are enormous, like a recipe book with millions of pages. Nobody can write a book that big alone. It takes many people, sometimes thousands, all working on the same recipe book.

And that's where things get messy.

Picture this. You're writing a story with your best friend. You each have a copy. You change the ending so the dragon becomes friends with the knight. At the same time, your friend changes the ending so the knight flies away on a giant eagle. Now you have two different endings. Which one is right? How do you put them together? And what if, a week later, you decide you liked the old ending better, but you already erased it?

Programmers had this problem all the time. So they invented a clever tool to help. It's called Git.

Git is like a magical save button. You know how in a video game you can save your progress, and if something goes wrong, you can go back to your last save? Git does that for code. Every time a programmer saves with Git, it takes a snapshot, like a photograph, of all their work at that exact moment. Later, they can look back through every single snapshot, all the way to the very beginning. Nothing is ever truly lost.

Git can do something even cooler. It lets people make their own separate copy of the project to try out ideas. This is called a branch. Think of a tree. The trunk is the main project. A branch grows off to the side, where you can experiment without hurting the trunk. If your idea works, you can join your branch back into the trunk. If it doesn't, no harm done. The trunk is safe.

Git was created in 2005 by a programmer from Finland named Linus Torvalds. He needed it to help thousands of people work together on a very important program he had started. Git worked wonderfully. But it had one problem. It was hard to use. It lived inside a plain black screen where you had to type special commands. And there wasn't one friendly place where everyone could meet and share their work.

So in 2008, a small group of programmers had an idea. What if they built a website, a home on the internet, where people could keep their Git projects, show them to others, and work on them together? They called it GitHub. "Git" for the tool, and "Hub" because a hub is a central meeting place, like the middle of a wheel where all the spokes come together.

Here's the best way to picture GitHub. Imagine a gigantic library. But instead of books that you can only read, every book is a project that you're allowed to look inside. You can see how it was made. You can see every change anyone ever made to it, and who made it, and when. And if you have a good idea, you can make your own copy, improve it, and then politely ask the owner, "Hey, I made this better. Would you like to add my changes?"

That polite asking is one of the most important ideas on GitHub. The owner can say yes, say no, or say, "Almost! Can you fix this one little part first?" It's like teamwork with good manners built right in.

Today, millions and millions of people use GitHub. Students use it for school projects. Big companies use it to build the apps you use every day. And regular people all over the world use it to work together on free programs that anyone can use, sometimes without ever meeting each other face to face.

So let's remember the big ideas from today. Code is a recipe that tells computers what to do. Git is a magical save button that remembers every version of your work and lets you try ideas on separate branches. And GitHub is the giant online library and meeting place where people store their projects and build things together.

In Part Two, we'll go back in time and meet the people who started GitHub. We'll learn how a tiny idea in 2008 grew into one of the most important places on the entire internet, and why one of the biggest companies in the world decided it wanted to buy it.

Until then, here's something to think about. If you could build anything with a team of friends from all around the world, what would you build?
