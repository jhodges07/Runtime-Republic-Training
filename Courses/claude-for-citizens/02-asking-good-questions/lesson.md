Welcome back to Runtime Republic. Last time we walked from Alan Turing to Claude. Today we get practical. How do you ask an AI assistant good questions, and how do you get answers you can trust?

Here is the one idea to remember: treat Claude like a very well-read new neighbor who just moved to town. Smart. Patient. Happy to help. But they do not know your county, your school district, or what happened at last month's commission meeting unless you tell them or ask them to look it up.

So the first habit is this: give it the context.

A vague question gets a vague answer. Instead of asking, "Why are my taxes so high?" try this: "I live in Sedgwick County, Kansas. My property tax went up this year. Explain, in plain English, the main parts of a Kansas property tax bill and who sets each part. Then tell me where I can find my county's official numbers." Same tool. Completely different answer. You told it where you live, what you want, and what form you want it in.

The second habit: ask for sources, and open them.

Claude can search the web and show you where its information came from. Ask for that every time it matters. "Give me the official source for that, with a link and a date." Then click the link and read the part it is talking about. Laws change. Budgets change. Tax rates change every year. An answer is only as good as the source behind it, and checking takes two minutes.

The third habit: ask for both sides.

On anything political, ask Claude to lay out the strongest arguments on more than one side. "What do supporters of this bill say? What do opponents say? What do the facts actually show?" A good citizen does not just collect arguments for what they already believe. You will be more persuasive, and harder to fool, if you understand the other side's best case.

The fourth habit: ask for plain language.

Government documents are written for lawyers. You are allowed to ask for something better. "Explain this like I'm new to it." "Define every term you use." "Give me the three most important points in one paragraph." There is no penalty for asking again, and no question is too basic.

The fifth habit: protect your privacy.

This one matters. Never type into any AI your Social Security number, bank or credit card numbers, passwords, or account numbers. If you want help understanding your tax statement, black out your name, address and account numbers first, or just type in the numbers that matter. And never share other people's private information. A good rule: if you would not post it publicly on Facebook, do not paste it into an AI.

The sixth habit: keep yourself in charge.

Claude can draft, summarize and suggest. You decide. If you use it to help write a message to your representative, the final words should be yours, and you should agree with every one of them. Elected officials can tell the difference between a form letter and a real constituent. Use AI to organize your thoughts, then speak in your own voice.

The seventh habit: keep your work in one place.

The Claude app has something called Projects. A project is like a folder with instructions. You can create one called "County Budget" and tell it, once, "I live in this county. Always cite official sources. Explain things in plain language." Every conversation inside that project starts with those instructions, so you do not have to repeat yourself.

Let's talk about what to expect.

Sometimes Claude will say it does not know, or that it cannot find something. That is a good sign, not a failure. An honest "I'm not sure" is far better than a confident wrong answer. And sometimes it will be wrong without knowing it. That is why habit number two, checking the source, is the most important habit in this whole series.

Let's recap. Give it the context. Ask for sources, and open them. Ask for both sides. Ask for plain language. Protect your privacy. Keep yourself in charge. And keep your work in one place.

If that list sounds less like computer tips and more like being a careful, fair-minded citizen, that is the point. AI rewards the habits good citizens already have.

In Episode 3, we turn these habits into real tasks: reading a bill, following your tax dollars, checking a viral post, and getting ready to contact your representative.
