Welcome to Runtime Republic. If you found us through Facebook or X, thank you for coming. This is a five-part series about one question: how can ordinary citizens use an AI assistant called Claude to understand their government, follow their tax dollars, and work together to improve things?

You do not need to be technical. You do not need to write code. If you can send a text message, you can do everything in this series.

Today is a short history. Where artificial intelligence came from, where Claude came from, and why that history matters to you as a citizen.

Let's start in 1950. A British mathematician named Alan Turing asked a simple question: can machines think? He proposed a test. If you talk to a machine through typed messages, and you cannot tell it apart from a person, maybe the question stops mattering. That idea has hung over the field ever since.

In the summer of 1956, a group of researchers met at Dartmouth College and gave the field its name: artificial intelligence. They thought a few smart people could solve it in a summer. They were wrong by about sixty years.

For decades, researchers tried to write down every rule by hand. If this, then that. Those systems worked in small, tidy situations and broke the moment real life got messy. Funding dried up more than once, in stretches people now call the AI winters.

There were headline moments. In 1997, IBM's Deep Blue beat the world chess champion. But Deep Blue could not answer a question about your property tax bill. It played chess.

The real change came from a different idea. Instead of writing the rules by hand, let the computer learn patterns from huge amounts of examples. Around 2012, that approach started winning, and by 2017 researchers had found a design, called the transformer, that could read a whole passage at once and understand how the words relate to each other.

Train that design on a large share of the world's written text, and something surprising happens. It does not just finish your sentence. It can summarize a long document, explain a hard idea in plain words, translate, and reason through a problem step by step. In late 2022, ChatGPT put that ability in front of the public, and the world noticed almost overnight.

Now, Claude.

Claude is made by a company called Anthropic, founded in 2021. From the start, its focus was safety. If these systems were going to become powerful, they should be honest, helpful, and hard to turn against people.

Here is the part that should interest every citizen. Anthropic trained Claude using an idea it calls Constitutional AI. Instead of relying only on people grading answers one at a time, it wrote down a set of principles, a constitution, and trained the model to check its own answers against those principles.

Think about that. Written principles, governing behavior, checked over and over. That is the same idea behind a constitutional republic. Power is supposed to follow written rules, and those rules are supposed to be checked. Whether you are talking about a government or a computer program, the idea is the same: write the rules down where everyone can see them, and hold the system to them.

Claude has grown quickly. The first version came out in 2023. Today it can read long documents, search the web and show you its sources, and help you organize your thinking. There are free and paid versions, and it works in a web browser and on your phone.

So why should a citizen care?

Three reasons.

First, government is mostly paperwork. Bills, budgets, audits, meeting minutes, tax statements. They are public, but they are long and written in a language most of us never learned. An AI assistant can turn a forty-page document into a plain-English summary in a minute, and then answer your follow-up questions. That puts something powerful in your hands: the ability to actually read what your government is doing.

Second, AI is a helper, not an authority. It is trained to be honest, and it will often tell you when it is unsure. But it can still be wrong, sometimes confidently. The history of AI is a history of systems that looked smart until they hit something new. So the habit we will teach in this series is simple: let AI help you find and understand, then check it yourself against the original source.

Third, the timing matters. For most of history, keeping a close eye on government took lawyers, accountants and staff. Today, a group of ordinary people with clear rules and good habits can do a lot of that work themselves, on their own time, from their own kitchen tables. The people who learn to work this way early will be the ones who teach everyone else.

That is where we are going.

In Episode 2, we get practical: how to ask Claude good questions, how to get answers you can trust, and what you should never type into any AI.
