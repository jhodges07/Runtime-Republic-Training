Welcome back to Runtime Republic. So far, we have talked about what you can do on your own with an AI assistant. Today is about what we can do together, and why Facebook and X, for all they are good at, are not the place to do it.

Let's be fair to social media first. Facebook and X are wonderful at finding people. They are how many of you found us. They are good for news, for conversation, and for spreading the word.

But try to make a decision on them, and things fall apart.

Posts scroll away. A good idea from Tuesday is buried by Thursday. The loudest voice gets the most attention, not the best idea. Nobody can see the current version of anything. Arguments repeat in a hundred different threads. And when something is finally decided, there is no record of who decided it, or why.

Imagine trying to write a law, or even plan a neighborhood cleanup, in a comment section. You would never finish.

People who build software ran into this same problem years ago. Thousands of strangers, all over the world, needed to work on the same project without stepping on each other. So they built a tool for it. It is called GitHub, and you do not need to be a programmer to use it.

Here is the simplest way to think about it. GitHub is a shared binder that never gets lost.

Each project gets its own binder, called a repository. Inside are the project's documents: its rules, its research, its drafts. Anyone allowed to see the binder can read every page. And every time a page changes, GitHub keeps a record of exactly what changed, who changed it, when, and why. Nothing is ever quietly erased. You can always look back.

Next to the binder is a suggestion box, called Issues. An issue is just a note that says, "Here's a question," or "Here's a problem," or "Here's an idea." Each issue gets a number, and everyone can comment on it in one place. Unlike a social media thread, it does not scroll away. It stays open until someone answers it or the group decides, and then it is closed with a note saying what happened.

And then there are proposals, called pull requests. A pull request says, "I suggest changing this page, from this, to this." Everyone can see the exact before and after, comment on it, and suggest improvements. Only when the people responsible for the project approve it does the change become part of the binder. It is a lot like an amendment being debated and voted on, except anyone can follow along from their phone.

That is it. A binder, a suggestion box, and proposals. You already understand GitHub.

Here is how to take your first step.

Step one: make a free GitHub account. You can do it at github.com, or download the GitHub app on your phone. Pick a username you would be comfortable having on public record, because your comments will carry it.

Step two: visit a project. Start by just reading. Look at the main page, which usually explains what the project is and how to help. Most projects have a page of rules for contributors. Read it. Every good project has rules, just like every good meeting has an agenda.

Step three: open your first issue. Find something small. A typo. A question about something you did not understand. A source you think would help. Open an issue, give it a clear title, and write a few sentences. Be specific and be kind. That is a real contribution, and the people running the project will be glad to see it.

Step four: join the conversation. Comment on other people's issues. Add a source. Answer a question you know about. Use what you learned in this series: before you post a fact, check the source and the date.

Where does Claude fit in? Everywhere. Claude can explain anything on GitHub you do not understand. "What is this page for?" "What does this word mean?" It can help you write a clear issue. It can read a long document in the project and summarize it for you. And as we said in Episode 2, you stay in charge. Claude helps you prepare; you post in your own words.

A few rules to keep it healthy. Never post private information, yours or anyone else's. Respect the project's rules and the people who keep it running. Disagree with ideas, never attack people. And remember that a public record is forever, so write what you would be proud to have your name on.

Let's recap. Social media is great for finding people and bad for deciding things. GitHub is a shared binder with a suggestion box and a way to propose changes. You can start today with a free account, by reading, and by opening one small, helpful issue.

In our final episode, we put it all together: how a group of citizens, each with their own AI assistant, can work on something important together without ever having to be in the same room at the same time.
