# What It Would Take Legally

A rule the Legislature can suspend by a vote will get suspended whenever it is inconvenient. So the one-hour rule has to live somewhere a single late-night vote cannot remove it. In the end, that means the Kansas Constitution.

There are three places the rule could live, and each has a tradeoff.

The first is the joint rules of the House and Senate. Each chamber adopts these by its own vote. That is the fastest path, and it could start as soon as next session. The weakness is that a rule can be set aside by vote. Today's thirty-minute rule for conference reports, Joint Rule Three F, can already be waived by two-thirds of the members present.

The second is a statute, a law passed by both chambers and signed by the Governor. A statute can bind state agencies, local governments and the Governor's office. But the Kansas Constitution says each house determines the rules of its own proceedings. So a court might not enforce a statute against the Legislature's internal steps.

The third is a constitutional amendment. That takes a vote of two-thirds of all the members of each chamber, and then a majority of the Kansans who vote on it. Once adopted, it cannot be waived by a vote, and a court could void any act that breaks it. The weakness is time. It is the slowest path, and it needs a statewide campaign.

A practical plan uses all three, in order.

First, adopt the one-hour rule as a joint rule. That proves it works and gives time to fix problems.

Second, pass a statute that does four things. It names the official repository and makes it the authoritative record of every bill's text. It amends the Open Meetings Act so that properly noticed, permanently logged discussion in the official repository counts as an open meeting, not a violation. It amends the Open Records Act so that every version and every comment is a permanent public record. And it applies the one-hour rule to the Governor's signature, and to any local governing body that chooses to adopt it.

Third, once the system has run for a session or two, put a constitutional amendment before the voters, so the rule can never be suspended.

There is reason to think the Legislature is open to this kind of change. In 2025, it updated both the Open Meetings Act and the Open Records Act. One new requirement says that any public body that live streams its meetings must make sure the public can actually watch. But that law said nothing about repositories or online forums. That is the gap this proposal fills.

Let's recap. A joint rule is fast but can be suspended. A statute reaches agencies and local governments, but may not reach the Legislature's own procedures. A constitutional amendment is slow, but it cannot be voted away. The practical path is all three in order: test it as a rule, write it into statute, then ask the voters to make it permanent.

In the final essay, we'll take on the hardest objections to the whole idea.
