Welcome back to Runtime Republic. So far, we have talked about how AI can help you in your own office. Today we look outward, at how the citizens you serve are starting to use these same tools, and how office holders can meet them where that work is happening.

Every office holder knows the old pattern. An issue catches fire on Facebook or X. Hundreds of comments, many angry, some accurate, some not. Then a crowded public meeting where people have three minutes each and everyone leaves frustrated. Social media is good at spreading alarm and finding allies. It is poor at producing a clear, sourced proposal you can actually act on.

Something different is starting to happen. Citizens are learning to use AI assistants to read the same budgets and bills you read. They are checking viral claims before they share them. And some are organizing on a platform called GitHub, which was built for large groups of people to work on the same documents together.

Here is what that looks like, in plain terms. A project on GitHub is a shared binder. Inside are the group's documents: its written rules, its research, its drafts. Every change is recorded: who made it, when, and why. Next to the binder is a list of issues, numbered questions and tasks that stay open until someone answers them. And changes come in as proposals, with a clear before and after, discussed in the open before they are accepted.

For an office holder, that is good news. Instead of five hundred comments, you might receive a single, organized proposal: here is the problem, here are the sources, here is what three other counties did, here is a draft, and here is a public record of how we got here and who disagreed. That is the kind of input you can work with.

So how do you meet citizens there? Here are five practical steps.

Step one: publish what people need, in forms they can use.

Many governments post budgets and audits as scanned images that neither people nor AI tools can read well. When you can, push for searchable documents and spreadsheets alongside the official versions. Clean public information is the cheapest transparency there is, and it makes every citizen's research more accurate.

Step two: watch, and learn the territory.

You do not have to join anything to read a public project. Look at the issues and proposals that concern your district. You will learn what citizens are worried about, what they have found, and where they are confused. Often the most useful thing you can do is answer a factual question with an official source.

Step three: respond in public, carefully.

If you comment on a public project, treat it like speaking at a meeting. Be accurate. Cite official documents. Say what you know, what you do not, and who in government can answer the rest. Remember the rules from Episode 2: your comments about official business may be public records, and conversations among a majority of your colleagues about a pending decision belong in a properly noticed public meeting, not in a comment thread.

Step four: point people to the official process.

Citizen projects can prepare proposals, research and draft language. Only your body can hold hearings, take testimony and vote. Tell people clearly how their work can enter the official process: which committee, which deadline, which public comment period. A well-organized proposal that arrives on time, through the right door, gets a real hearing.

Step five: respect the difference between input and decision.

Some proposals will arrive with impressive research and wide support. Some will be wrong. Your job is not to rubber-stamp the best-organized group, and it is not to dismiss them either. Weigh the evidence, hear from people who disagree, and decide. Then explain your decision, in the same open spirit the citizens used.

A few cautions. Do not assume an online group speaks for your whole district; many constituents will never join one. Do not share non-public information in public projects. And stay civil, especially when you disagree. Citizens are watching how officials respond, and a respectful answer to a hard question teaches more than any lesson can.

Let's recap. Social media spreads alarm; organized projects produce proposals. Publish usable information. Watch and learn. Respond in public, carefully. Point people to the official process. And keep input and decision distinct.

In our final episode, we put it together: what government looks like when citizens and office holders can work on the same problems, in the open, on their own schedules, with every decision still made by the people accountable for it.
