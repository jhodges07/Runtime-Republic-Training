Welcome back to Runtime Republic. Last time we looked at what an AI assistant can do for someone in public office, and where its limits are. Today is the most important episode in this series for anyone who holds office: how to use AI without stumbling into a records problem, an open-meetings problem, or a breach of your constituents' trust.

A quick note first. This is not legal advice. The examples here use Kansas law, and every state is different. Before you use AI for official business, talk with your clerk, your body's attorney, or your legislative staff, and follow any written policy your body has adopted.

Rule one: assume it is a public record.

In Kansas, the Open Records Act was amended in 2016. Since then, records about official business can be public records even when they sit on a personal phone or in a private email account. The test is the subject, not the device. So assume that a conversation with an AI assistant about official business may be a record someone can request, subject to the law's exceptions. Write every prompt as if a reporter might read it someday, because one might. That is not a reason to avoid the tool. It is a reason to use it professionally.

Rule two: do not let AI become a back channel.

Kansas's Open Meetings Act covers more than meetings in a room. Under the law, a series of communications, such as emails, calls or messages, can count as a meeting if together they involve a majority of the body's members, share a common topic of the body's business, and are meant to reach agreement on something that needs a binding vote. Now picture an AI summary of an issue, with your suggested position, forwarded from one member to the next until a majority has weighed in. That can look a lot like a meeting held outside public view. Use AI to prepare yourself. Do your deliberating with your colleagues in public, where it belongs.

Rule three: protect the people who contact you.

Constituents tell you things they would not tell anyone else. Their health. Their finances. A dispute with a neighbor. A problem with a child's school. Never paste a constituent's name, address, account numbers, case details or other personal information into an AI tool unless your body has approved that tool for that purpose and you understand how the data is stored. When you want help with a sensitive case, describe it in general terms with names and identifying details removed. The question to ask yourself: would this person be comfortable seeing what I just typed?

Rule four: check every fact before it leaves your office.

We said it last time, and it bears repeating. AI can be confidently wrong, especially about statute numbers, dollar amounts and dates. Ask it to give you its sources, with links and dates, and then open them. If you cite a figure in a meeting, a newsletter or a floor speech, you should be able to point to the official document it came from. Your credibility is the most valuable thing you have in office. Do not lend it to a machine.

Rule five: the decision and the words are yours.

AI can draft a constituent reply, an op-ed, or talking points. But you are the one who answers to voters. Read every word before it goes out under your name, change anything that does not sound like you, and never send something you do not fully agree with. Some officials choose to say when they have used AI to help draft a document. Whether or not your body requires that, being open about your process builds trust.

Rule six: ask for every side.

Office holders represent everyone in the district, including people who did not vote for them. Make it a habit to ask, "What are the strongest arguments for and against this proposal? Who would be helped, and who would be harmed?" A good assistant will lay out more than one side fairly. That will not make your decision for you, but it will make you harder to surprise.

Rule seven: write your own rules down.

The simplest protection is a short written policy, for yourself or your office. Which tools you use. What information never goes into them. How you check facts. How you keep records. If your body has not adopted a policy yet, this is a good thing to raise, in public, at a meeting. Written rules, followed and checked, are the same idea behind good government, and behind the way Claude itself was trained.

Let's recap. Assume it is a public record. Do not let AI become a back channel. Protect the people who contact you. Check every fact. Keep the decision and the words your own. Ask for every side. And write your rules down.
