Welcome to Runtime Republic. This series is for people who hold public office, or plan to. State representatives and senators. County commissioners. City council members. School board members. Whatever your party, and whatever the size of your district, this series is about one question: how can an AI assistant called Claude help you serve your constituents better, without giving up any of the judgment and accountability that come with the office?

You do not need to be technical. If you can send an email, you can do everything in this series.

Today is a short history, and an honest look at what this tool is and is not.

The idea of a thinking machine goes back to 1950, when the mathematician Alan Turing asked whether a machine could carry on a conversation well enough to pass for a person. Six years later, researchers at Dartmouth College named the field artificial intelligence. For decades after, they tried to write down every rule by hand, and their systems kept breaking the moment real life got messy.

The breakthrough came when researchers stopped writing rules and let computers learn patterns from enormous amounts of text. By 2017, they had a design that could read a whole document at once and understand how its parts relate. Trained on a large share of the world's writing, these systems learned to summarize, explain, compare and draft. In late 2022, ChatGPT put that ability in front of the public.

Claude is made by Anthropic, a company founded in 2021 with a focus on safety. It trained Claude using what it calls Constitutional AI: a written set of principles the model learns to check its own answers against. Every office holder will recognize the idea. You took an oath to a constitution. Written rules, governing the use of power, checked over and over. Whatever you think of artificial intelligence, the principle behind this one should feel familiar.

So what does this mean for someone in public office?

Start with the job itself. Much of public service is reading. Bills and amendments. Fiscal notes. Budget books hundreds of pages long. Audit reports. Agenda packets that arrive a few days before a meeting. Constituent letters. Staff memos. Testimony. Nobody elected you to skim, but there are only so many hours in a week, especially for the many local officials who serve part time with small staffs or none at all.

An AI assistant changes the math on that reading. Give it a sixty-page agenda packet and ask, "Which items spend money, how much, and from which fund?" Give it a bill and its fiscal note and ask, "What would this change, who does it affect, and what questions should I ask the sponsor?" In a minute or two you have a map of the document, so you can spend your own time on the parts that matter.

It can also help you explain. Constituents deserve to understand why you voted the way you did. Claude can help you turn a technical decision into a clear, honest paragraph a busy family can read on their phone.

Now the limits, because they matter more for you than for almost anyone.

First, AI can be wrong. It is trained to be honest and will often say when it is unsure, but it can still state something confidently that is not true, especially about laws, numbers and dates that change. A wrong number in a citizen's Facebook post is a mistake. A wrong number in a public official's statement is a news story. So the rule is simple: never repeat a fact from an AI without checking the original source.

Second, AI is not a decision maker. It has no oath, no district and no accountability. It can lay out options, arguments and trade-offs. It cannot weigh your constituents' interests, and it cannot be voted out. Every decision stays yours.

Third, public office comes with rules ordinary citizens do not face. Open records laws. Open meetings laws. Privacy duties to the people who contact you. Those rules apply to how you use AI too, and we will spend all of Episode 2 on them.

Here is why this matters now. Citizens are starting to use these same tools to read your budgets, follow your votes, and draft their own proposals. Some of them will arrive at your meetings better prepared than ever. That is good for self-government, and it is a reason for office holders to learn the tools too. An official who understands how citizens are using AI can meet them halfway: publishing information in forms people can actually use, and answering well-organized questions with well-organized answers.

None of this is partisan. Clear reading, honest explanations, careful checking and open records serve every constituent, in every district, under every party label.

In Episode 2, we cover using AI responsibly in public office: what becomes a public record, how to avoid an accidental open-meetings problem, and how to protect the people who trust you with their information.
